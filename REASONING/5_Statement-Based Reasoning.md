
## 1. Statement and Conclusion (कथन और निष्कर्ष)

A **statement** provides information. A **conclusion** is something that logically follows from that information.

In the exam, determine whether the conclusion necessarily follows from the statement. Do not use outside assumptions.

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Statement:** All teachers are educated. Ravi is a teacher.

**Conclusions:**

I. Ravi is educated.

II. All educated people are teachers.

**Answer:** Only conclusion I follows.

**Explanation:** Ravi belongs to the group of teachers, and all teachers are educated. However, the statement does not say that every educated person is a teacher.

**Example 2**

**Statement:** Some students are athletes. All athletes are physically active.

**Conclusions:**

I. Some students are physically active.

II. All students are physically active.

**Answer:** Only conclusion I follows.

**Explanation:** The students who are athletes must be physically active. Nothing establishes that every student is physically active.

**Example 3**

**Statement:** No laptop is a desktop computer.

**Conclusion:** Some laptops are desktop computers.

**Answer:** The conclusion does not follow.

**Explanation:** The conclusion contradicts the statement.

### Quick Rules for Conclusions

|Statement|Meaning|
|---|---|
|All A are B|Every A belongs to B|
|No A is B|A and B do not overlap|
|Some A are B|At least one A is B|
|Some A are not B|At least one A is outside B|

**Exam trick:** “All A are B” does not mean “All B are A.”

## 2. Statement and Assumption (कथन और पूर्वधारणा)

An **assumption** is an unstated idea that the speaker takes for granted when making a statement or proposal.

Ask yourself: _What must be accepted, at least implicitly, for this statement to make sense?_

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Statement:** “Use a helmet while riding a motorcycle to reduce the risk of serious head injury.”

**Assumption:** Wearing a helmet can help reduce head injuries.

**Answer:** The assumption is implicit because the recommendation relies on the protective benefit of a helmet.

**Example 2**

**Statement:** “The school has introduced extra mathematics classes to help students improve their scores.”

**Assumptions:**

I. Extra mathematics classes may help students improve.

II. Every student has already failed mathematics.

**Answer:** Only assumption I is implicit.

**Explanation:** Extra classes are being offered to improve performance. The statement does not say that every student has failed.

**Example 3**

**Statement:** “Buy this phone because it has a long battery life.”

**Possible assumption:** Battery life is a factor that may influence a buyer's decision.

**Answer:** The assumption is reasonable and implicit in the recommendation.

**Exam trick:** An assumption is not simply a fact that could be true. It should be an unstated idea underlying the statement or recommendation.

## 3. Statement and Argument (कथन और तर्क)

An **argument** gives a reason for or against a proposal. You must decide whether the reason is strong, relevant, and important.

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Question:** Should schools teach basic computer literacy to all students?

**Argument I:** Yes, digital skills are useful for education and many everyday tasks.

**Argument II:** No, computers are usually available only in large cities.

**Answer:** Argument I is strong; Argument II is weak.

**Explanation:** The first argument directly addresses the usefulness of computer education. The second makes a broad claim that does not establish why computer literacy should not be taught.

**Example 2**

**Question:** Should traffic rules be enforced strictly?

**Argument I:** Yes, enforcement can discourage dangerous driving and improve road safety.

**Argument II:** No, traffic rules are printed in small letters in some places.

**Answer:** Argument I is strong; Argument II is weak.

**Explanation:** The first gives a relevant safety reason. The second does not meaningfully address the need for enforcement.

## 4. Statement and Course of Action (कथन और कार्यवाही)

A **course of action** is a proposed step to solve a problem or respond to a situation.

A good course of action should be relevant, practical, proportionate, and likely to address the problem.

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Statement:** Several students are becoming ill after drinking water from a school water cooler.

**Courses of action:**

I. Stop using the suspected water supply and arrange safe drinking water.

II. Ignore the complaints until the end of the school term.

**Answer:** Only course of action I is appropriate.

**Explanation:** It addresses a potential health risk immediately. Ignoring the complaints could expose more students to harm.

**Example 2**

**Statement:** A city experiences frequent road accidents at a particular junction.

**Courses of action:**

I. Investigate the causes and assess suitable safety measures.

II. Close every road in the city permanently.

**Answer:** Only course of action I is appropriate.

**Explanation:** Investigating the problem is proportionate and evidence-based. Permanently closing every road is an excessive response.

**Example 3**

**Statement:** A company repeatedly loses data because employees do not back up their files.

**Courses of action:**

I. Introduce a reliable backup system and train employees.

II. Stop all employees from using computers.

**Answer:** Only course of action I is appropriate.

**Explanation:** It directly addresses the cause without unnecessarily stopping essential work.

**Remember:** A course of action should not be selected merely because it sounds strict or decisive. It must actually address the stated problem.

## 5. Cause and Effect (कारण और प्रभाव)

These questions ask you to determine whether two events are causally related or merely occur together.

### Common Possibilities

- Statement I is the cause and Statement II is its effect.
    
- Statement II is the cause and Statement I is its effect.
    
- Both statements are effects of a common cause.
    
- Both are independent events.
    
- A causal relationship cannot be established from the information given.
    

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Statement I:** Heavy rainfall continued throughout the night.

**Statement II:** Several streets were flooded the following morning.

**Answer:** Statement I is a plausible cause of Statement II.

**Explanation:** Sustained heavy rainfall can cause street flooding. In a reasoning question, use the stated context and the most direct relationship.

**Example 2**

**Statement I:** A city experienced unusually high temperatures.

**Statement II:** Sales of cold drinks increased.

**Answer:** Statement I may be the cause of Statement II.

**Explanation:** Hot weather can increase demand for cold drinks, although other factors may also affect sales.

**Example 3**

**Statement I:** A person bought a new laptop.

**Statement II:** A nearby shop reduced its prices.

**Answer:** No direct causal relationship can be established from these statements alone.

**Explanation:** The events could be unrelated. Their occurrence at around the same time does not prove causation.

**Important distinction:** Correlation does not necessarily mean causation. Avoid inventing a causal link that the question does not support.

## 6. Assertion and Reason (अभिकथन और कारण)

An assertion–reason question presents two statements:

- **Assertion (A):** A claim or fact.
    
- **Reason (R):** An explanation that may or may not correctly explain the assertion.
    

You usually need to evaluate two things separately:

1. Is the assertion true?
    
2. Is the reason true, and does it correctly explain the assertion?
    

### Standard Answer Options

|Option|Meaning|
|---|---|
|A|Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A|
|B|Both A and R are true, but R does not correctly explain A|
|C|A is true, but R is false|
|D|A is false, but R is true|
|E|Both A and R are false|

**Note:** Follow the option format specified in the actual question, as some exams use four options instead of five.

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Assertion:** Plants need sunlight for photosynthesis.

**Reason:** Sunlight provides energy used during photosynthesis.

**Answer:** Option A — Both are true, and R correctly explains A.

**Example 2**

**Assertion:** The Earth revolves around the Sun.

**Reason:** The Moon revolves around the Earth.

**Answer:** Option B — Both are true, but R does not explain A.

**Example 3**

**Assertion:** All prime numbers are even.

**Reason:** The number 2 is an even prime number.

**Answer:** Option D — Assertion is false, but reason is true.

**Explanation:** Only one prime number, 2, is even. Other primes such as 3, 5 and 7 are odd.

## 7. Strong and Weak Arguments (मजबूत और कमजोर तर्क)

This is closely related to statement-and-argument questions, but the focus is on how convincing and relevant the given reason is.

- **Strong argument:** Directly relevant, logical, and supported by a reasonable basis.
    
- **Weak argument:** Irrelevant, based mainly on emotion or an unsupported generalisation, or fails to address the question.
    

### Solved Examples

**Example 1**

**Question:** Should public libraries provide digital resources?

**Argument I:** Yes, digital resources can help users access information when physical copies are unavailable.

**Argument II:** No, the colour of library walls may affect how books look.

**Answer:** Argument I is strong; Argument II is weak.

**Example 2**

**Question:** Should workplaces conduct fire-safety drills?

**Argument I:** Yes, drills help employees practise evacuation procedures.

**Argument II:** No, drills take time away from normal work.

**Answer:** Argument I is strong. Argument II is generally weak as an argument against drills because it ignores the important safety benefit, though time and cost can be relevant considerations when planning their frequency.

### Strong vs Weak: Quick Comparison

|Strong Argument|Weak Argument|
|---|---|
|Directly answers the question|Irrelevant to the question|
|Uses logical reasoning|Relies on unsupported claims|
|Considers practical consequences|Makes extreme generalisations|
|Gives a meaningful benefit or risk|Depends mainly on personal preference|

## 8. Fast-Solving Strategy for the Exam

|Question Type|Key Question to Ask|
|---|---|
|Statement and conclusion|Must this conclusion follow from the statement?|
|Statement and assumption|What unstated idea is the speaker relying on?|
|Statement and argument|Is this reason relevant and logically convincing?|
|Course of action|Will this step address the problem practically and proportionately?|
|Cause and effect|Is there a credible direct causal link, or are these events merely associated?|
|Assertion and reason|Are both statements true, and does the reason explain the assertion?|
|Strong and weak arguments|Is the argument relevant, logical, and convincing?|

### Final Revision Points

1. Do not assume information that is not provided in the statement.
    
2. “All A are B” does not mean “All B are A.”
    
3. An assumption must be implicit in the statement, not merely possible.
    
4. A strong argument must address the actual question.
    
5. A course of action must be practical and proportionate.
    
6. Two events occurring together does not prove that one caused the other.
    
7. In assertion–reason questions, evaluate the truth of A and R separately before checking the explanatory relationship.
    
8. Read every option carefully; similar wording can change the answer.
