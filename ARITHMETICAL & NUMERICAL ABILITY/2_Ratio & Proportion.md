
## 1. Basic Ratio

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division.

Example: There are 20 boys and 15 girls.

Boys : Girls = 20 : 15 = 4 : 3

A ratio a : b can also be written as a / b.

### Important Rules

- Convert quantities to the same unit before comparing them.
    
- Simplify a ratio by dividing both terms by their HCF.
    
- The order matters: 2 : 3 is not the same as 3 : 2.
    
- A ratio has no unit after both quantities are expressed in the same unit.
    

### Example 1: Simplify 48 : 72

HCF of 48 and 72 = 24.

48 : 72 = (48 / 24) : (72 / 24) = 2 : 3

Answer: 2 : 3

### Example 2: Divide ₹600 in the Ratio 2 : 3

Total parts = 2 + 3 = 5

First share = (2 / 5) × 600 = ₹240

Second share = (3 / 5) × 600 = ₹360

**Shortcut:** If a total T is divided in the ratio a : b:

- First share = (a × T) / (a + b)
    
- Second share = (b × T) / (a + b)
    

## 2. Equivalent Ratios

Equivalent ratios represent the same relationship. Multiply or divide both terms by the same non-zero number.

Example:

2 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 6 : 9 = 10 : 15

### Example: Find x if 5 : 8 = x : 40

Since 8 × 5 = 40, multiply the first term by 5 as well.

x = 5 × 5 = 25

Answer: 25

Equivalent ratios are useful when comparing quantities, scaling recipes, and solving proportion questions.

## 3. Proportion

A proportion states that two ratios are equal.

a : b = c : d

Equivalent form: a / b = c / d

**Key rule: Product of extremes = Product of means.**

a × d = b × c

In a : b :: c : d:

- Extremes: a and d
    
- Means: b and c
    

### Example: Find x if 4 : 7 = 20 : x

4 / 7 = 20 / x

Cross-multiplication:

4 × x = 7 × 20

4x = 140

x = 140 / 4 = 35

Answer: 35

**Exam trap:** In a / b = c / d, cross-multiplication gives a × d = b × c, not a × c = b × d.

## 4. Direct Proportion

Two quantities are in direct proportion when they increase or decrease together in the same ratio, assuming the rate remains constant.

### Examples

- More notebooks cost more money at the same price per notebook.
    
- More distance requires more fuel if mileage remains constant.
    
- More workers may complete more work in the same time, assuming equal productivity and divisible work.
    

### Formula

x1 / x2 = y1 / y2

Equivalent form: y = k × x

Here, k is a constant.

### Example 1: Cost of Pens

If 5 pens cost ₹60, what is the cost of 8 pens?

More pens mean greater cost.

5 / 8 = 60 / x

5x = 480

x = 480 / 5 = ₹96

Answer: ₹96

### Example 2: Distance and Time

A car travels 150 km in 3 hours at a constant speed. How far will it travel in 7 hours?

Speed = 150 / 3 = 50 km/h

Distance = 50 × 7 = 350 km

Answer: 350 km

**Shortcut:** In direct proportion, if one quantity doubles, the other also doubles.

## 5. Inverse Proportion

Two quantities are in inverse proportion when one increases and the other decreases so that their product remains constant.

### Examples

- More workers require less time for the same fixed work, assuming equal efficiency.
    
- Greater speed means less travel time for the same distance.
    
- More taps with equal flow rates fill a tank faster.
    

### Formula

x1 × y1 = x2 × y2

Equivalent form: y = k / x

Here, k is a constant.

### Direct vs. Inverse Relationship

|Input x|Direct output y = 2 × x|Inverse output y = 2 / x|
|---|---|---|
|0.5|1|4|
|1|2|2|
|1.5|3|1.33|
|2|4|1|
|2.5|5|0.8|

In direct proportion, output divided by input stays constant.

In inverse proportion, input multiplied by output stays constant.

### Example 1: Workers and Days

Six workers complete a job in 12 days. How many days will 8 workers take, assuming equal efficiency?

Workers increase, so days decrease. This is inverse proportion.

6 × 12 = 8 × x

x = 72 / 8 = 9 days

Answer: 9 days

### Example 2: Speed and Time

A car takes 6 hours at 40 km/h to cover a distance. How long will it take at 60 km/h?

40 × 6 = 60 × x

x = 240 / 60 = 4 hours

Answer: 4 hours

### Direct vs. Inverse Proportion

|Feature|Direct proportion|Inverse proportion|
|---|---|---|
|Relationship|Both change in the same direction|Change in opposite directions|
|Constant|y / x = k|x × y = k|
|If one doubles|The other doubles|The other halves|
|Example|Quantity and cost at a fixed price|Workers and time for fixed work|

**Common trap:** More workers do not always mean less time if the total work also increases. Check that the total work is fixed.

## 6. Partnership

Partnership questions calculate how profits or losses are divided among partners.

### Case 1: Equal Investment Time

If partners invest different amounts for the same duration:

Profit ratio = Investment ratio

Example: A invests ₹20,000 and B invests ₹30,000 for one year. Total profit is ₹25,000.

A : B = 20,000 : 30,000 = 2 : 3

Total parts = 2 + 3 = 5

A's profit = (2 / 5) × 25,000 = ₹10,000

B's profit = (3 / 5) × 25,000 = ₹15,000

### Case 2: Different Investment Durations

When both investment amount and time differ:

Profit ratio = Capital × Time ratio

Example: A invests ₹10,000 for 12 months, while B invests ₹15,000 for 8 months. Total profit is ₹18,000.

A's capital × time = 10,000 × 12 = 120,000

B's capital × time = 15,000 × 8 = 120,000

Profit ratio = 1 : 1

Each partner receives:

18,000 / 2 = ₹9,000

Answer: ₹9,000 each.

**Exam trap:** If a partner joins later or withdraws money early, calculate how many months each investment remains in the business.

## 7. Mixture-Related Ratio

Mixture questions often involve combining two substances, liquids, or ingredients in different ratios.

### Type A: Combining Quantities

Example: Milk and water are mixed in the ratio 3 : 2. If the mixture contains 25 litres, find the quantity of each.

Total parts = 3 + 2 = 5

Milk = (3 / 5) × 25 = 15 litres

Water = (2 / 5) × 25 = 10 litres

Answer: 15 litres of milk and 10 litres of water.

### Type B: Mixing Two Solutions

Example: A 20-litre solution contains 30% acid. How much acid does it contain?

Acid = (30 / 100) × 20 = 6 litres

The remaining 14 litres are other components, assuming the solution contains only acid and one other component.

### Type C: Replacing Liquid in a Container

If a container contains V litres of a mixture and r litres are removed and replaced with another liquid, the original liquid remaining after one replacement is:

Original liquid remaining = V × (1 − r / V)

Example: A 20-litre container contains pure milk. Remove 5 litres and replace it with water. How much milk remains?

Milk remaining = 20 × (1 − 5 / 20)

= 20 × 3 / 4

= 15 litres

Water added = 5 litres

Resulting milk : water ratio = 15 : 5 = 3 : 1

For repeated replacements, the remaining original liquid after n operations is:

Remaining original liquid = V × (1 − r / V)^n

This assumes the mixture is uniform and the same volume is removed and replaced each time.

## 9. Last-Minute Revision

- Ratio compares two quantities.
    
- Equivalent ratios are obtained by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number.
    
- Proportion means two ratios are equal.
    
- Direct proportion: both quantities change in the same direction.
    
- Inverse proportion: one quantity increases while the other decreases.
    
- Partnership profit depends on capital × time.
    
- Mixture questions often use ratios to calculate component quantities.
    
- Always check units, total parts, and whether the relationship is direct or inverse.
