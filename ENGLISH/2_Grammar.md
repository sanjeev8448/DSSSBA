## 1. Error detection

In error-detection questions, a sentence may be divided into parts. You must identify the part containing the grammatical error, or choose “No error” if the sentence is correct.

### Common error patterns

|Incorrect sentence|Correct sentence|Rule|
|---|---|---|
|He go to school daily.|He goes to school daily.|Subject–verb agreement|
|She do not like tea.|She does not like tea.|Use does with he/she/it|
|He is senior than me.|He is senior to me.|Correct preposition|
|I have seen him yesterday.|I saw him yesterday.|Finished past time|
|She is an European citizen.|She is a European citizen.|European begins with a /y/ sound|
|Each of the boys have a book.|Each of the boys has a book.|Each takes a singular verb|
|He returned back home.|He returned home.|Redundant wording|
|One of my friend is a doctor.|One of my friends is a doctor.|One of takes a plural noun|

### How to solve error detection

1. Identify the subject and main verb.
    
2. Check tense and time expressions.
    
3. Check articles, prepositions and pronouns.
    
4. Check singular/plural agreement.
    
5. Read the whole sentence for meaning and correct usage.
    

Exam tip: Do not assume every sentence contains an error. Some questions include “No error” as an option.

## 2. Subject–verb agreement

The verb must agree with its subject in number and person.

### Core rules

|Rule|Example|
|---|---|
|Singular subject takes a singular verb in the present simple|He works hard.|
|Plural subject takes a plural verb|They work hard.|
|Each, every, either, neither generally take singular verbs|Each student is ready.|
|Two subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb|Ravi and Amit are friends.|
|With either…or and neither…nor, the verb usually agrees with the nearer subject|Neither the teacher nor the students are present.|
|Uncountable nouns generally take singular verbs|The information is useful.|
|A number of takes a plural verb|A number of students are absent.|
|The number of takes a singular verb|The number of students is increasing.|
|Amounts treated as one unit take a singular verb|Ten kilometres is a long distance.|
|Collective nouns can take singular or plural verbs depending on meaning and usage|The committee has reached a decision.|

### Special cases

Rule A — Words between the subject and verb do not change agreement.

The bouquet of roses is beautiful.

The subject is bouquet, not roses.

Rule B — Along with, as well as, together with do not normally make the subject plural.

The principal, along with the teachers, is attending the meeting.

Rule C — There is / There are

- There is a book on the table.
    
- There are two books on the table.
    

Rule D — One of

One of the players has scored a century.

### Quick comparison

- Neither of the answers is correct.
    
- Both answers are correct.
    
- Everyone has completed the task.
    
- The police are investigating the case.
    

Remember: words such as everyone, somebody, nobody are grammatically singular, while police is generally plural in standard usage.

## 3. Tenses

Tense indicates when an action occurs. English has three main time categories: present, past and future. Each is commonly studied in four forms.

|Tense|Structure|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Present simple|Subject + V1(s/es)|She writes daily.|
|Present continuous|is/am/are + V-ing|She is writing now.|
|Present perfect|has/have + V3|She has written a letter.|
|Present perfect continuous|has/have been + V-ing|She has been writing for an hour.|
|Past simple|Subject + V2|She wrote yesterday.|
|Past continuous|was/were + V-ing|She was writing at 5 PM.|
|Past perfect|had + V3|She had written before I arrived.|
|Past perfect continuous|had been + V-ing|She had been writing for an hour before dinner.|
|Future simple|will + V1|She will write tomorrow.|
|Future continuous|will be + V-ing|She will be writing at 5 PM.|
|Future perfect|will have + V3|She will have written it by Friday.|
|Future perfect continuous|will have been + V-ing|By noon, she will have been writing for two hours.|

### Important tense rules

1. Present simple for habits and general truths

- He plays cricket every Sunday.
    
- The Earth revolves around the Sun.
    

2. Present continuous for actions happening now

- They are studying at present.

3. Present perfect with unfinished time or present relevance

- I have completed my work.
    
- She has lived here for five years. (She still lives here, in this intended meaning.)
    

Use past simple with a finished past-time expression:

- Incorrect: I have met him yesterday.
    
- Correct: I met him yesterday.
    

4. Since and for

- Since indicates the starting point: since 2020, since Monday.
    
- For indicates duration: for three years, for two hours.
    

5. Past perfect for the earlier of two past actions

When the sequence needs clarification:

- The train had left before we reached the station.

6. Future time clauses

After when, before, after, until, as soon as in a future time clause, use the present simple rather than will:

- Correct: I will call you when I arrive.
    
- Incorrect: I will call you when I will arrive.
    

## 4. Articles — A, An, The

Articles are used before nouns.

### A. A and an

Use a or an for a singular countable noun when it is not specifically identified.

The choice depends on the initial sound, not simply the first letter.

|Use a|Use an|
|---|---|
|a university|an umbrella|
|a European country|an honest person|
|a one-rupee coin|an hour|
|a useful book|an MBA graduate|

The u in university has a /y/ sound, while the h in honest and hour is silent.

### B. The

Use the when the noun is specific, identifiable or unique in context.

- The book on the table is mine.
    
- The Sun rises in the east.
    
- The first chapter is easy.
    
- The Ganga is a major river.
    

### C. Zero article

Do not normally use an article before plural or uncountable nouns when speaking generally.

- Books are useful.
    
- Water is essential for life.
    
- Mathematics is an important subject.
    

Compare:

- Children need education. (Children generally)
    
- The children in this class are studying. (Specific children)
    

### D. Common article errors

- Incorrect: He is best player in the team.
    
- Correct: He is the best player in the team.
    
- Incorrect: She gave me an advice.
    
- Correct: She gave me some advice.
    
- Incorrect: I play the cricket.
    
- Correct: I play cricket.
    

## 5. Prepositions

Prepositions express relationships of time, place, direction and other connections.

### A. Prepositions of time

|Preposition|Usage|Example|
|---|---|---|
|At|Exact time|at 6 PM|
|On|Days and dates|on Monday; on 15 August|
|In|Months, years, long periods|in October; in 2026|
|Since|Starting point|since 2022|
|For|Duration|for four years|
|By|Deadline|Finish by Friday.|
|Until|Continuation up to a point|Wait until noon.|

### B. Prepositions of place

- At: at the station, at the entrance.
    
- In: in Delhi, in a room, in India.
    
- On: on the table, on the wall.
    
- Between: between two people or clearly distinct entities.
    
- Among: among members of a group.
    

### C. Frequently tested combinations

|Expression|Correct usage|
|---|---|
|Good at|She is good at mathematics.|
|Interested in|He is interested in science.|
|Depend on|Success depends on effort.|
|Afraid of|She is afraid of heights.|
|Prefer X to Y|I prefer tea to coffee.|
|Senior to|He is senior to me.|
|Different from|This book is different from that one.|
|Married to|She is married to a doctor.|
|Consist of|The team consists of eleven players.|
|Insist on|He insisted on paying.|

Exam trap: Standard exam English generally uses prefer X to Y, not prefer X than Y.

## 6. Modals

Modals are auxiliary verbs that express ability, permission, possibility, obligation, advice and other meanings.

|Modal|Common meaning|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Can|Ability or informal permission|I can swim.|
|Could|Past ability or polite request|Could you help me?|
|May|Permission or possibility|May I enter?|
|Might|Possibility|It might rain.|
|Must|Strong obligation or deduction|You must wear a helmet.|
|Should|Advice or expectation|You should revise daily.|
|Ought to|Advice or duty|You ought to apologise.|
|Will|Future or willingness|I will help you.|
|Would|Polite request or hypothetical action|Would you open the door?|
|Shall|Offers or suggestions in suitable contexts|Shall we begin?|
|Need|Necessity, in modal constructions|You need not worry.|
|Dare|Courage, in certain modal constructions|Dare he question the decision?|

### Rules to remember

- Modal + base verb: She can swim, not can swims.
    
- Modal + not: You should not delay.
    
- Modal + have + V3: He might have forgotten.
    
- Must not expresses prohibition; need not means something is not necessary.
    
- Should have + V3 often expresses a past duty or regret: You should have called me.
    
- Could have + V3 can express a past possibility or an unrealised opportunity.
    

Examples:

- You must not enter. (Prohibited)
    
- You need not enter. (Entering is unnecessary)
    

## 7. Active and passive voice

Voice shows whether the subject performs an action or receives it.

- Active: The teacher explains the lesson.
    
- Passive: The lesson is explained by the teacher.
    

### Basic structure

Active: Subject + verb + object.

Passive: Object + suitable form of be + V3 + (by + subject).

|Tense|Active|Passive|
|---|---|---|
|Present simple|She writes a letter.|A letter is written by her.|
|Present continuous|She is writing a letter.|A letter is being written by her.|
|Present perfect|She has written a letter.|A letter has been written by her.|
|Past simple|She wrote a letter.|A letter was written by her.|
|Past continuous|She was writing a letter.|A letter was being written by her.|
|Past perfect|She had written a letter.|A letter had been written by her.|
|Future simple|She will write a letter.|A letter will be written by her.|
|Modal|She can write a letter.|A letter can be written by her.|

### Important rules

1. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence.
    
2. Preserve the tense by changing the form of be.
    
3. Use the past participle (V3) in the passive.
    
4. The by-phrase can be omitted when the doer is unknown or unimportant.
    
5. A sentence without a suitable object generally cannot be changed into the ordinary passive form.
    

Example:

- Active: Someone stole my phone.
    
- Passive: My phone was stolen.
    

## 8. Direct and indirect speech (narration)

Direct speech quotes the speaker's exact words. Indirect speech reports the meaning without quoting the exact words.

- Direct: He said, “I am tired.”
    
- Indirect: He said that he was tired.
    

### A. Common tense changes

When the reporting verb is in the past, and the situation calls for backshift:

|Direct speech|Indirect speech|
|---|---|
|Present simple|Past simple|
|Present continuous|Past continuous|
|Present perfect|Past perfect|
|Past simple|Past perfect (often in traditional exam rules)|
|Will|Would|
|Can|Could|
|May|Might|

Example:

Direct: She said, “I have finished my work.”

Indirect: She said that she had finished her work.

### B. Pronoun and time changes

Pronouns change according to who is speaking and who is being addressed. Time expressions change when the reporting context requires it.

|Direct|Possible indirect form|
|---|---|
|this|that|
|these|those|
|now|then|
|today|that day|
|yesterday|the previous day|
|tomorrow|the following day|
|here|there|
|ago|before|

### C. Reporting questions

Direct: He said to me, “Are you ready?”

Indirect: He asked me if I was ready.

Direct: She said, “Where do you live?”

Indirect: She asked where I lived.

Remember:

- Yes/no questions use if or whether.
    
- Wh-questions retain the question word.
    
- Indirect questions use statement word order, not question word order.
    

### D. Commands and requests

Direct: The teacher said to me, “Open the book.”

Indirect: The teacher told me to open the book.

Direct: He said to me, “Please help me.”

Indirect: He requested me to help him.

### E. Universal truths

Universal truths generally remain in the present tense.

Direct: The teacher said, “The Earth revolves around the Sun.”

Indirect: The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.

## 9. Pronouns

A pronoun replaces a noun or noun phrase.

|Type|Examples|Example sentence|
|---|---|---|
|Personal|I, we, he, she, they|She is studying.|
|Possessive|mine, yours, hers, ours|This book is mine.|
|Reflexive|myself, yourself, himself|He hurt himself.|
|Demonstrative|this, that, these, those|These are mine.|
|Relative|who, whom, whose, which, that|The boy who won is my friend.|
|Interrogative|who, whom, whose, which, what|Who is there?|
|Indefinite|someone, anyone, everybody|Everybody is ready.|
|Reciprocal|each other, one another|They helped each other.|

### Important exam rules

- Subject pronouns: I, he, she, we, they.
    
- Object pronouns: me, him, her, us, them.
    
- Possessive determiner before a noun: This is my book.
    
- Possessive pronoun without a following noun: This book is mine.
    
- Reflexive pronouns are used when the subject and object refer to the same person: She taught herself.
    
- In traditional exam grammar, who is used for a subject and whom for an object.
    

Examples:

- Incorrect: Me and Rahul went to school.
    
- Correct: Rahul and I went to school.
    
- Incorrect: This is her's.
    
- Correct: This is hers.
    
- Incorrect: Each student must bring their books. (Some traditional tests may expect a singular pronoun.)
    
- Exam-safe traditional form: Each student must bring his or her book.
    

Note that singular they is accepted in modern English; follow the convention used by the particular exam question.

## 10. Adjectives

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun.

Examples: a tall building, an honest person, a difficult question.

### Degrees of comparison

|Degree|Example|
|---|---|
|Positive|tall|
|Comparative|taller|
|Superlative|tallest|

Examples:

- Rahul is tall.
    
- Rahul is taller than Amit.
    
- Rahul is the tallest boy in the class.
    

### Important rules

Comparative degree + than

She is taller than her sister.

Superlative degree usually takes the

He is the most intelligent student in the class.

Avoid double comparatives and superlatives

- Incorrect: more better
    
- Correct: better
    
- Incorrect: most tallest
    
- Correct: tallest
    

Irregular comparisons

|Positive|Comparative|Superlative|
|---|---|---|
|Good|Better|Best|
|Bad|Worse|Worst|
|Little|Less|Least|
|Many/much|More|Most|
|Far|Farther/further|Farthest/furthest|

### Order of adjectives

A useful general order is opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose → noun.

Example: a beautiful small old wooden box.

## 11. Adverbs

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or sometimes a whole sentence.

- Verb: He runs quickly.
    
- Adjective: She is very intelligent.
    
- Adverb: He runs extremely quickly.
    
- Sentence: Fortunately, nobody was injured.
    

### Common types

|Type|Question answered|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Manner|How?|She spoke politely.|
|Time|When?|He arrived yesterday.|
|Place|Where?|Come here.|
|Frequency|How often?|I always exercise.|
|Degree|To what extent?|It is very cold.|

### Common traps

- Hard = with effort; hardly = almost not.
    
- Late = after the expected time; lately = recently.
    
- Good is an adjective; well is usually an adverb of manner.
    
- Too can mean excessively; very means to a high degree.
    
- Enough usually comes after an adjective/adverb: strong enough. Before a noun: enough time.
    

Examples:

- He works hard. (He puts in effort.)
    
- He hardly works. (He does very little work.)
    
- She sings well. (Not usually sings good in standard grammar.)
    

## 12. Conjunctions

A conjunction connects words, phrases, or clauses.

### A. Coordinating conjunctions

The common coordinating conjunctions are FANBOYS:

|Conjunction|Meaning|Example|
|---|---|---|
|For|Reason|I stayed home, for I was unwell.|
|And|Addition|She sings and dances.|
|Nor|Negative alternative|He neither called nor wrote.|
|But|Contrast|He is poor but honest.|
|Or|Alternative|Hurry up or you will miss the bus.|
|Yet|Contrast|He is young, yet experienced.|
|So|Result|It was raining, so we stayed inside.|

### B. Subordinating conjunctions

These connect a dependent clause to a main clause.

|Conjunction|Example|
|---|---|
|Although / though|Although it rained, we played.|
|Because|I stayed home because I was ill.|
|If|If you study, you will improve.|
|Unless|Unless you hurry, you will be late.|
|While|She cooked while I studied.|
|Before / after|Wash your hands before you eat.|
|Since|Since you are here, let us begin.|
|As soon as|Call me as soon as you arrive.|

Important: Unless generally means if not. Avoid adding another negative unnecessarily.

- Incorrect: Unless you do not study, you will fail. (Not the intended meaning in standard exam usage.)
    
- Correct: Unless you study, you will fail.
    

### C. Correlative conjunctions

These work in pairs.

|Pair|Example|
|---|---|
|Either…or|Either Ravi or Amit will attend.|
|Neither…nor|Neither Ravi nor Amit was present.|
|Both…and|Both Riya and Priya are here.|
|Not only…but also|She is not only intelligent but also hardworking.|
|Whether…or|Whether you agree or disagree, the rule applies.|

Maintain parallel grammatical structure:

- Incorrect: She likes not only reading but also to write.
    
- Better: She likes not only reading but also writing.
    

### D. Common conjunction errors

- Although…yet: In standard exam constructions, avoid using although and yet together redundantly.
    
- Because…so: Do not normally use both to express the same cause-and-result relationship.
    
- No sooner…than: No sooner had he arrived than it began to rain.
    
- Hardly/scarcely…when: Hardly had I reached home when the phone rang.
    
- As…as: She is as tall as her sister.
