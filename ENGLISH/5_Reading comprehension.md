## 1. Reading Comprehension

Reading comprehension tests your ability to understand a passage, identify its main idea, draw conclusions, and interpret vocabulary in context.

### Types of questions

|Question type|What to identify|
|---|---|
|Main idea|The central message of the passage|
|Supporting detail|A fact explicitly mentioned|
|Inference|A conclusion supported by the passage|
|Vocabulary in context|The meaning of a word as used in the passage|
|Tone|The author's attitude, such as critical, optimistic, or neutral|
|Title|A concise heading that captures the main idea|
|Fact vs opinion|Verifiable information versus a judgment or belief|

### Practice passage

Read the passage carefully.

> Technology has changed the way people learn. Online resources allow students to access educational material at almost any time and from many locations. However, access to information alone does not guarantee learning. Students must evaluate the reliability of sources, practise regularly, and apply what they learn. Teachers continue to play an important role by guiding students, clarifying difficult concepts, and encouraging critical thinking. Therefore, effective education often combines digital resources with thoughtful instruction and active participation.

Questions

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
    
2. Why is access to information alone insufficient?
    
3. What role do teachers continue to play?
    
4. What does “reliability” most nearly mean in this context?
    
5. Which title best suits the passage?
    

### Solving strategy

- Read the questions first if the passage is long and time is limited.
    
- Find the relevant sentence in the passage before selecting an answer.
    
- For inference questions, choose what the passage supports—not what you personally believe.
    
- Avoid extreme options containing words such as always, never, or only unless the passage clearly supports them.
    
- For the title, choose the option that covers the whole passage rather than one minor detail.
    

## 2. Cloze Test

A cloze test is a passage containing blanks. You must select the word that best fits each blank grammatically and logically.

### Example

Regular exercise is essential (1) ___ good health. It helps people (2) ___ fit and reduces the risk of certain diseases. However, exercise should be performed (3) ___ a suitable routine. People should also pay attention (4) ___ their diet. A balanced lifestyle can (5) ___ overall well-being.

|Blank|Options|Correct answer|
|---|---|---|
|1|for / at / by / with|for|
|2|stay / stays / stayed / staying|stay|
|3|according to / despite / unless / because|according to|
|4|on / to / at / for|to|
|5|improve / improvement / improved / improving|improve|

### Four checks for every blank

1. Grammar: Does the option fit the tense, subject, and sentence structure?
    
2. Collocation: Do the words naturally occur together, such as pay attention to?
    
3. Meaning: Does the completed sentence make sense?
    
4. Context: Does the word fit the overall passage, not just the isolated sentence?
    

Common traps: choosing a grammatically possible word that does not fit the meaning, confusing similar prepositions, and overlooking singular/plural agreement.

Spelling questions test commonly confused words, silent letters, doubled consonants, and correct word formation.

### Rules and memory tricks

- I before E: believe, piece, achieve; but there are exceptions, such as receive and ceiling.
    
- Double consonants: accommodation has double c and double m; embarrass has double r and double s.
    
- Silent letters: knowledge (silent k), doubt (silent b), island (silent s).
    
- Word endings: necessary, ordinary, stationery, and dictionary.
    
- Common suffixes: -tion in education; -sion in decision; -ance in importance.
    

### Spelling-question strategy

When options contain similar spellings, compare the exact letter sequence rather than relying only on how the word sounds. Practise writing difficult words from memory and revisiting the ones you miss.

## 4. Punctuation

Punctuation clarifies meaning and separates ideas. Questions may ask you to select the correctly punctuated sentence or identify an error.

### Essential punctuation marks

|Mark|Use|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Full stop `.`|Ends a statement|The class has started.|
|Comma `,`|Separates list items or introductory elements|After lunch, we studied.|
|Question mark `?`|Ends a direct question|Where are you going?|
|Exclamation mark `!`|Expresses strong emotion|What a wonderful result!|
|Apostrophe `'`|Shows possession or contraction|Rahul's book; don't|
|Colon `:`|Introduces a list or explanation|Bring three items: a pen, a ruler, and a notebook.|
|Semicolon `;`|Joins closely related independent clauses|It was late; we went home.|
|Quotation marks `" "`|Mark direct speech or quotations|She said, "I am ready."|
|Hyphen `-`|Joins certain compound words|A well-known author|
|Dash `—`|Marks a break or additional information|One thing mattered most—honesty.|

### Important rules

Rule 1: Separate independent clauses correctly.

Incorrect: I was tired, I went to bed.

Correct: I was tired, so I went to bed.

You can also write: I was tired; I went to bed.

Rule 2: Use apostrophes correctly.

- `Student's book` — belonging to one student.
    
- `Students' books` — belonging to multiple students.
    
- `It's raining` — contraction of it is.
    
- `The dog wagged its tail` — possessive form, without an apostrophe.
    

Rule 3: Use commas with introductory phrases.

Correct: After finishing the examination, she left the hall.

Rule 4: Do not use a question mark for an indirect question.

- Direct: He asked, "Where are you going?"
    
- Indirect: He asked where I was going.
    

Rule 5: Punctuation can change meaning.

- Let's eat, Grandma! — speaking to Grandma.
    
- Let's eat Grandma! — changes the meaning dramatically.
    

## 5. Active and Passive Voice

Active voice focuses on the person or thing performing an action. Passive voice focuses on the person or thing receiving the action.

Active voice

The teacher explains the lesson.

Subject → verb → object

Passive voice

The lesson is explained by the teacher.

Object → suitable form of “be” → past participle (V3) → optional “by” phrase

### Tense conversion table

|Tense|Active voice|Passive voice|
|---|---|---|
|Present simple|She writes a letter.|A letter is written by her.|
|Present continuous|She is writing a letter.|A letter is being written by her.|
|Present perfect|She has written a letter.|A letter has been written by her.|
|Past simple|She wrote a letter.|A letter was written by her.|
|Past continuous|She was writing a letter.|A letter was being written by her.|
|Past perfect|She had written a letter.|A letter had been written by her.|
|Future simple|She will write a letter.|A letter will be written by her.|
|Modal verb|She can write a letter.|A letter can be written by her.|

### Conversion procedure

1. Identify the object of the active sentence.
    
2. Move it to the subject position.
    
3. Use the correct form of be for the original tense.
    
4. Change the main verb to its past participle (V3).
    
5. Add by + agent when the doer is important.
    

Example:

Active: The police arrested the thief.

- Object: the thief
    
- Tense: past simple
    
- Passive: The thief was arrested by the police.
    

### Special cases

Imperative sentences

- Active: Open the door.
    
- Passive: Let the door be opened.
    

A common alternative is: You are requested to open the door, when the context is a polite request.

Modal verbs

- Active: You must follow the rules.
    
- Passive: The rules must be followed.
    

Questions

- Active: Did she complete the task?
    
- Passive: Was the task completed by her?
    

When passive voice is not normally possible

Intransitive verbs without an object generally cannot form an ordinary passive.

- Active: He slept.
    
- No ordinary passive transformation is possible.
    

## 6. Direct and Indirect Speech

Direct speech reproduces the speaker's exact words. Indirect (reported) speech reports the meaning without quoting the exact words.

Direct speech

She said, "I am tired."

Indirect speech

She said that she was tired.

### A. Common tense changes

When the reporting verb is in the past, the tense commonly shifts back if the context requires it.

|Direct speech|Indirect speech|
|---|---|
|Present simple: "I work."|He said that he worked.|
|Present continuous: "I am working."|He said that he was working.|
|Present perfect: "I have worked."|He said that he had worked.|
|Past simple: "I worked."|He said that he had worked.|
|Past continuous: "I was working."|He said that he had been working.|
|Will: "I will work."|He said that he would work.|
|Can: "I can work."|He said that he could work.|
|May: "I may work."|He said that he might work.|

These changes are common examination patterns, not absolute rules. A tense may remain unchanged when the statement is still true or the context does not require backshift.

### B. Changes in time and place

|Direct speech|Indirect speech (when context shifts)|
|---|---|
|Now|Then|
|Today|That day|
|Yesterday|The previous day / the day before|
|Tomorrow|The next day / the following day|
|Here|There|
|This|That|
|These|Those|
|Ago|Before|
|Last week|The previous week|
|Next week|The following week|

Pronouns also change according to who is speaking and who is being addressed. These changes depend on context.

### C. Questions

Yes/no question: Use if or whether, and change the word order to a statement.

- Direct: He said to me, "Are you ready?"
    
- Indirect: He asked me if I was ready.
    

Wh-question: Keep the question word but use statement word order.

- Direct: She said, "Where do you live?"
    
- Indirect: She asked where I lived.
    

Incorrect: She asked where did I live.

### D. Commands and requests

Use told/ordered/asked/requested + object + to-infinitive.

- Direct: The teacher said to me, "Sit down."
    
- Indirect: The teacher told me to sit down.
    
- Direct: He said to me, "Please help me."
    
- Indirect: He requested me to help him. (More natural: He asked me to help him.)
    

For a negative command, use not to + base verb.

- Direct: He said, "Do not touch it."
    
- Indirect: He told me not to touch it.
    

### E. Exclamations and universal truths

- Direct: He said, "What a beautiful place!"
    
- Indirect: He exclaimed that it was a very beautiful place.
    

- Direct: The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
    
- Indirect: The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
    

Universal truths generally retain the present tense.
